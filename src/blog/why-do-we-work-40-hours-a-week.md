Somewhere along the way, "nine to five, five days a week" started to feel like a law of nature, like gravity or Mondays. It isn't. The 40-hour week is a fairly recent invention, and the people who fought for it were not aiming at a perfect number a scientist had discovered.

This piece explains where the 40-hour week came from, why a famous economist thought we would be working 15 hours by now, what the evidence says about long hours, and how to use all of it to make smarter decisions about your time and money.

> **Short answer:** We work 40 hours a week because of a historical compromise, not a scientific optimum. Labour movements spent decades fighting for an eight-hour day ("eight hours for work, eight hours for rest, eight hours for what we will"), big employers like Ford adopted a five-day week, and governments eventually wrote limits into law. In the US, the Fair Labor Standards Act set a maximum week (44 hours at first, 40 soon after) with extra pay above it. Forty stuck because it was legally anchored, culturally sticky, and convenient for employers, not because 40 is the magic figure for human productivity.

## Where did the 40-hour week actually come from?

It came from workers clawing back hours, not from anyone calculating an ideal. For most of human history nobody worked "hours" at all. Work was task-based and seasonal: you planted when it was time to plant, harvested when it was time to harvest, and the length of your day bent around daylight, weather, and what needed doing. Selling your time in standardised, clock-measured blocks is a strange and recent idea.

That changed with factories. Once work moved indoors, around machines that ran on a clock rather than the sun, employers could and did push the working day to brutal lengths. Twelve, fourteen, even sixteen hours were not unusual in the early industrial era. The modern workweek is, in large part, the story of workers winning those hours back.

### The eight-hour dream

The rallying cry came early. A slogan associated with the reformer Robert Owen in the early 1800s split the day into three equal parts: "eight hours labour, eight hours recreation, eight hours rest." Decades later, labour organisers were marching behind a snappier version that National Geographic records from the American eight-hour movement: "eight hours for work, eight hours for rest, and eight hours for what we will."

Three eights. It is a tidy idea, and notice what it is based on: a fair division of the *day*, not a study of when output peaks. The target was a human life with room in it, not an efficiency curve.

### Ford, the Depression, and the law

Three things turned the slogan into a standard. First, some large employers moved ahead of the law. The Library of Congress records that Ford Motor Company announced a five-day week in September 1926; a company statement declared that "Five working days of eight hours, with no overtimes, is to constitute a week's work." Ford argued, self-interestedly but not wrongly, that rested workers with free time and wages were both more productive and better customers.

Second, the Great Depression made shorter hours look like a cure for mass unemployment: if everyone worked fewer hours, the available work could be shared among more people. The American Economic Association's summary of research by Price Fishback, Chris Vickers, and Nicolas Ziebarth describes how the short-lived President's Reemployment Agreement pushed firms toward much shorter weeks. As Vickers put it, "there are plenty of firms with 50- to 60-hour workweeks before the PRA, and that just goes away in a month." That episode helped nudge the economy toward the 40-hour norm.

Third came the law. The US Fair Labor Standards Act set a federal maximum workweek, starting at 44 hours and stepping down to 40 soon after. Crucially, it required a premium for extra hours: the Department of Labor states that covered "employees must receive overtime pay for hours worked over 40 in a workweek at a rate not less than time and one-half their regular rates of pay." That overtime rule matters more than it looks, and we will come back to it.

## Why isn't 40 hours a scientific number?

Because nobody ever proved it was. Forty is the sediment left behind by a century of bargaining between workers who wanted their lives back and employers who wanted output. The figure that won was the one that was politically and legally achievable, and then it froze into "normal."

You can see it is a compromise because the number changes depending on where you stand. One of the earliest international standards, the International Labour Organization's very first convention, set a limit of "eight in the day and forty-eight in the week" for industrial work. In the European Union, the Working Time Directive caps *average* weekly working time, including overtime, at 48 hours over a reference period, with various opt-outs and national rules layered on top. Plenty of countries, workplaces, and professions sit above or below 40 as their day-to-day reality.

So is a 40-hour week "normal"? It is a common anchor in many richer economies, yes. But treat it as a convention you have inherited, not a verdict about how much a human should work. If 40 were a natural law, it would not keep needing a law to hold it in place.

## Did Keynes really predict a 15-hour week?

Yes, and he was half right. In a 1930 essay, "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren," the economist John Maynard Keynes made a bold guess: that within about a hundred years the economic problem "may be solved, or be at least within sight of solution." People, he suggested, might get by on "three-hour shifts or a fifteen-hour week," mostly to keep themselves occupied.

The productivity part of his bet was roughly right. We did get dramatically richer and more efficient, and working hours fell a long way. Economic historians Michael Huberman and Christopher Minns, assembling comparable long-run estimates for a dozen countries, put French annual hours at roughly 3,168 at the start of their series and about 1,443 by the end. Across the sample the working year shrank enormously. But the fall stopped nowhere near Keynes's 15-hour week. So what happened to the rest of the freed-up time?

We spent it. Not literally, but close. As we got richer, our sense of "enough" climbed right along with our incomes. Keynes himself saw the risk: he split human wants into absolute needs, which can be satisfied, and relative needs, "those which satisfy the desire for superiority," which exist only because they make us feel ahead of other people. Those second ones, he wrote, "may indeed be insatiable; for the higher the general level, the higher still are they." The bigger house, the newer phone, the nicer trip: as soon as they are normal, they stop feeling like luxuries and start feeling like the baseline.

That is exactly the pattern we call [lifestyle creep]({{BASE}}blog/lifestyle-creep/): each raise quietly becomes the new normal, so the extra money buys a bigger life rather than a shorter work year. We took our productivity gains mostly as *more stuff* instead of *more time*. Keynes's grandchildren could have chosen the 15-hour week. We largely chose the bigger shopping cart.

There is a romantic counter-story that says ancient hunter-gatherers already had something like the 15-hour week, the "original affluent society" that anthropologist Marshall Sahlins described. It is a genuinely debated claim: Sahlins argued that foragers in easy environments met their needs in strikingly few hours, while later time-use work tends to find more work once you count food processing, tool-making, and childcare. Treat the "early humans only worked a few hours" line as contested, not settled.

## Do longer hours actually produce more?

Not past a point, and often not by much. If 40 is not scientific, you would at least hope it is efficient. The honest answer from the evidence is that long hours are far less productive than they feel, especially at the top end.

The sharpest single study is by the economist John Pencavel, who looked at historical records of British munitions workers, most of them women, during the First World War. He found the relationship between output and hours was not a straight line. His own summary: "below an hours threshold, output is proportional to hours; above a threshold, output rises at a decreasing rate as hours increase." In that data the knot sat at about 49 hours a week. Below it, an extra hour of work bought almost an extra hour of output (an estimated elasticity of 1.002). Above it, the same extra hour bought barely anything (an elasticity of 0.172). The tired hours at the end of a long week produced surprisingly little.

Modern experiments point the same way. In the UK's largest four-day-week trial, coordinated by Autonomy with a research team from Boston College and Cambridge, 61 companies and around 2,900 staff moved to a shorter week for the same pay. Afterwards, 56 of the 61 companies (92%) kept the four-day week, revenue across the reporting firms "stayed broadly the same" and rose 1.4% on average by company size, 71% of employees reported lower burnout, and resignations fell by 57%. Fewer hours did not wreck output, which is hard to square with the idea that 40-plus is the productive sweet spot.

So if very long hours are a poor deal, why does 40-plus persist? A few forces hold it in place.

- **The law is an anchor.** Once overtime pay kicks in above a set line, that line becomes the default shape of a "full-time" job. Employers design roles around it, and everyone calibrates to it.
- **Fixed costs per person.** Hiring, training, benefits, and management attention cost roughly the same whether someone works 30 hours or 45. That tempts employers to load more hours onto each existing head rather than hire more people.
- **Culture and identity.** Busyness reads as importance. Long hours can feel like proof you matter, even when they are just proof you are tired.

None of this means work is bad. Work is also meaning, structure, and a paycheque. The point is narrower: the specific number 40 is a convention, and the "more hours equals more worth" instinct is not backed by the evidence.

## How do you price your life in hours?

Divide any price by what an hour of your life is actually worth after the costs of working, and you see its true cost: time, not money. This is where the history becomes personal and genuinely useful. If your week is a fixed pile of hours that you trade for money, then every price tag has a hidden second number: how much of your life it costs. The idea was popularised by the book *Your Money or Your Life* by Vicki Robin and Joe Dominguez, which reframes money as "life energy" you have traded your hours for.

Most people think their hourly rate is "salary divided by paid hours." It is lower than that, usually by a lot, once you count what the job actually costs you in time and money.

### The Real-Hour Rule

Use this simple test to find your true hourly wage and then price things in it.

1. **Start with take-home pay**, the money that actually lands in your account, not the headline salary.
2. **Subtract the costs of working.** Commute fares or fuel, the "work wardrobe," bought lunches, the extra childcare that exists only because you are at work, and the decompression spending (the takeaway you order because you are too wiped to cook). These are real costs of the job.
3. **Divide by all the hours work eats.** Not just paid hours: add the commute, the getting-ready, the checking-email-in-bed, and the recovery time you need to be fit for the next day.
4. **That number is your real hourly wage.** Now flip it around: for any purchase, divide the price by your real hourly wage to see its price in life-hours.

Say Sam (our standard friendly example) takes home a 25-an-hour wage on paper. After the commute, the smart clothes, the sad desk salads, and the "I earned this" spending, Sam's real rate might be closer to 15 an hour. These are just illustrative numbers, but the gap is the point. A gadget priced at 300 is not "a bit of this month's pay." It is **20 hours of Sam's actual life**, half a working week, gone.

### Scoring two job offers in real-hours

Headline salary is a poor way to compare jobs because it ignores everything the job quietly takes. Here is our own quick scoring grid: run both offers through it, and the "smaller" salary often wins.

|---|---|---|---|

Fill it in honestly and you are no longer comparing two salaries. You are comparing two hourly rates for your life, which is the comparison that counts.

Once you see purchases and jobs that way, two things happen. Impulse buys lose some of their shine, because "two days of my life" is a stickier price than a number on a screen. And you stop quietly overpaying on the boring recurring stuff, because every unnecessary subscription or lazy renewal is now measured in hours, not pennies. Overpaying out of inertia is its own trap; it is a cousin of the [loyalty penalty]({{BASE}}blog/loyalty-penalty/), where staying put quietly costs you.

## Common myths and mistakes

- **Myth: 40 hours is scientifically optimal.** It is a bargained standard with a legal anchor, not a productivity finding. The evidence on very long hours actually points the other way.
- **Myth: more hours always means more done.** Past a threshold, output per hour falls. Pencavel's munitions data found extra hours above about 49 a week bought very little. You can be busier and produce less.
- **Myth: Keynes was just wrong.** He was largely right that we would get far richer and work fewer hours; he underestimated how fast our wants would expand to absorb the gains.
- **Mistake: judging a job only by its salary.** A higher salary with a punishing commute and expensive "keeping up appearances" costs can be a worse real-hourly deal than a smaller one nearby. Do the Real-Hour maths before you compare.
- **Mistake: treating your hours as infinite.** Time is the one budget you cannot top up. Spending it as if it were free is the most expensive habit there is.
- **Mistake: assuming your hours are legally fixed everywhere.** Rules on maximum hours, overtime, and flexible or part-time work vary a lot by country and employer. It is worth knowing what actually applies to you rather than guessing.

## What to do: put your hours to work

You cannot vote yourself a 15-hour week this afternoon. But you can change how you value and spend the hours you have. Here is a practical order of operations.

1. **Calculate your real hourly wage.** Run the Real-Hour Rule above once, properly, with honest numbers. Most people are quietly shocked, which is exactly the useful part.
2. **Convert your next big purchase into hours.** Before buying anything significant, divide the price by your real hourly wage. Then ask: is this thing worth that many hours of my life? Sometimes yes. Now it is a decision, not a reflex.
3. **Decide what extra hours are actually for.** If you take on overtime or a side hustle, name the goal before you start: a specific buffer, a debt cleared, a trip. Hours traded for a vague "more" tend to vanish into lifestyle creep. For more on how bright people still end up with nothing to show for good incomes, see [why smart people stay broke]({{BASE}}blog/why-smart-people-stay-broke/).
4. **Build a stash of "freedom money."** A cash buffer is not just insurance; it is leverage. Savings big enough to walk away for a while give you standing to negotiate hours, refuse unpaid overtime, or wait for a better role.
5. **Ask about flexible or compressed hours.** Four longer days, a nine-day fortnight, part-time, or remote arrangements can buy back real time for the same or similar pay. The four-day-week trial above suggests the output cost is often smaller than managers fear. The worst outcome of asking is usually a polite no.

## Key terms

- **40-hour work week:** The convention of full-time work as roughly eight hours a day over five days, anchored in law by overtime rules rather than set by any productivity study.
- **Fair Labor Standards Act:** The US federal law that set a maximum standard workweek and required premium pay (time-and-a-half) for hours worked beyond it.
- **Overtime premium:** The higher pay rate, commonly 1.5 times the normal wage, owed for hours above the legal threshold; it makes long hours costlier for employers and anchors the "full-time" line.
- **Eight-hour movement:** The nineteenth and early twentieth century labour campaign for a working day of eight hours, captured in the slogan "eight hours for work, eight hours for rest, eight hours for what we will."
- **Working Time Directive:** The EU rule capping average weekly working time, including overtime, at 48 hours over a reference period, subject to opt-outs and national variation.
- **Real hourly wage:** Your take-home pay minus the costs of working, divided by every hour the job consumes, including commute, preparation, and recovery, not only paid hours.
- **Life energy:** The idea from *Your Money or Your Life* that money represents hours of your life traded away, so prices can be read as a cost in time.
- **Lifestyle creep:** The tendency for spending and expectations to rise with income, so raises fund a bigger life instead of fewer working hours.
- **Original affluent society:** Marshall Sahlins's contested claim that hunter-gatherers met their needs with few working hours, disputed by later time-use research.

## Frequently asked questions

### Who invented the 40-hour work week?

No single person did. It grew out of decades of labour campaigning for an eight-hour day, was adopted early by some large employers (Ford's five-day week is the famous example), and was eventually written into law. In the US, the Fair Labor Standards Act set the federal maximum and the overtime rule that cemented 40 as the norm.

### When did the 40-hour work week begin?

As a widespread legal standard, it dates to the late 1930s in the US: the Fair Labor Standards Act set a maximum week, 44 hours initially and dropping to 40 soon after, with premium pay above it. The idea is older, campaigned for through the 1800s and adopted by some firms in the 1920s, but that law is the usual answer to "when did it start."

### Is a 40-hour work week normal around the world?

It is a common anchor in many wealthier economies, but it is far from universal. An early International Labour Organization convention set eight hours a day and 48 a week for industry, while the EU's Working Time Directive caps average weekly hours at 48 including overtime. Actual hours worked vary widely by country, industry, and individual.

### Did Keynes really predict a 15-hour work week?

Yes. In his essay "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren," Keynes suggested that within roughly a century, rising wealth and productivity could reduce work to "three-hour shifts or a fifteen-hour week." The productivity gains largely arrived; the leisure mostly did not. The common explanation is that our spending and our sense of "enough" expanded along with our incomes.

### Are longer working hours more productive?

Generally no, especially at the extremes. John Pencavel's study of munitions workers found that beyond about a 49-hour week, extra hours added very little output. Modern four-day-week trials point the same way: firms that cut hours for the same pay mostly kept output and revenue steady. Fatigue drags down output per hour, so long hours can feel productive while producing little.

### How do I calculate the "real" hourly value of my time?

Take your actual take-home pay, subtract the money the job costs you (commuting, work clothes, bought lunches, work-driven childcare, decompression spending), then divide by *all* the hours work consumes, including commute, prep, and recovery, not only paid hours. The result is your real hourly wage, which you can then use to price purchases in hours of your life.

### Can a company stay productive on fewer hours?

Often, yes. In the UK's largest four-day-week pilot, most participating firms kept the shorter week afterwards, average revenue held roughly steady, and reported burnout and resignations fell. Results vary by industry and depend on redesigning the work, but the evidence undercuts the assumption that fewer hours automatically means less output.
