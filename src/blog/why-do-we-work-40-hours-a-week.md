Somewhere along the way, "nine to five, five days a week" started to feel like a law of nature, like gravity or Mondays. It isn't. The 40-hour week is a fairly recent invention, and the people who fought for it were not aiming at a perfect number a scientist had discovered. This piece explains where the 40-hour week really came from, why a famous economist thought we would be working 15 hours by now, and how to use that history to make smarter decisions about your own time and money.

> **Short answer:** We work 40 hours a week because of a historical compromise, not a scientific optimum. Labour movements spent decades fighting for an eight-hour day ("eight hours for work, eight hours for rest, eight hours for what we will"), big employers like Ford adopted a five-day week, and governments eventually wrote limits into law: in the US, the Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938 set a maximum week (44 hours at first, 40 shortly after) with extra pay above it. Forty stuck because it was legally anchored, culturally sticky, and convenient for employers, not because 40 is the magic figure for human productivity.

## Where the 40-hour week actually came from

For most of human history, nobody worked "hours" at all. Work was task-based and seasonal: you planted when it was time to plant, harvested when it was time to harvest, and the length of your day bent around daylight, weather, and what needed doing. The idea of selling your time in standardised, clock-measured blocks is strange and new.

That changed with factories. Once work moved indoors, around machines that ran on a clock rather than the sun, employers could and did push the working day to brutal lengths. Twelve, fourteen, even sixteen hours were not unusual in the early industrial era. The modern workweek is, in large part, the story of workers clawing those hours back.

### The eight-hour dream

The rallying cry came early. A slogan associated with the reformer Robert Owen in the early 1800s split the day into three equal parts: "eight hours labour, eight hours recreation, eight hours rest." Decades later, labour organisers were marching behind a snappier version: "eight hours for work, eight hours for rest, eight hours for what we will."

Three eights. It is a tidy idea, and notice what it is based on: a fair division of the *day*, not a study of when output peaks. The target was a human life with room in it, not an efficiency curve.

### Ford, the Depression, and the law

Two things turned the slogan into a standard. First, some large employers moved ahead of the law. By the 1920s, Henry Ford's plants were running a five-day, 40-hour week, and Ford argued (self-interestedly, but not wrongly) that rested workers with free time were both more productive and better customers.

Second, the Great Depression made shorter hours look like a solution to mass unemployment: if everyone worked fewer hours, the thinking went, the available work could be shared among more people. In the US, the short-lived President's Reemployment Agreement pushed firms toward much shorter weeks, and researchers studying that period have argued it helped nudge the economy toward the 40-hour norm. Then came the law. The Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938 set a federal maximum workweek, starting at 44 hours and stepping down to 40 soon after, and required employers to pay a premium (time-and-a-half) for hours beyond it.

That last detail matters more than it looks, and we will come back to it.

| Era | Typical working time | What drove it |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-industrial | Task-based, seasonal, sun-bound | Weather, harvests, daylight |
| Early industrial | 12-16 hour days common | Factory clocks, cheap labour |
| Late 1800s to early 1900s | The eight-hour day, fought for | Labour movements, strikes |
| 1920s | Five-day, 40-hour weeks at some big firms | Employer experiments (e.g. Ford) |
| 1938 onward (US) | 40-hour legal standard with overtime pay | Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938 |

## A compromise, not a magic number

Here is the part worth sitting with: nobody ever proved that 40 is the right number. It is the sediment left behind by a century of bargaining between workers who wanted their lives back and employers who wanted output. The figure that won was the one that was politically and legally achievable, then it froze into "normal."

You can see it is a compromise because the number changes depending on where you stand. One of the earliest international standards, the International Labour Organization's very first convention, aimed at eight hours a day and 48 hours a week in industry. In the European Union, the Working Time Directive caps *average* weekly working time at 48 hours including overtime (with various opt-outs and national rules on top). Plenty of countries, workplaces, and professions sit above or below 40 as their day-to-day reality.

So is a 40-hour week "normal"? It is a common anchor in many richer economies, yes. But treat it as a convention you have inherited, not a verdict about how much a human should work. If 40 were a natural law, it would not keep needing a law to hold it in place.

## The 15-hour week that never showed up

In a 1930 essay called "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren," the economist John Maynard Keynes made a bold guess. Within about a hundred years, he reckoned, societies would be so much richer and more productive that the big economic problem would be essentially solved. People might work, he suggested, something like three-hour shifts or a 15-hour week, mostly to keep themselves occupied.

The productivity part of his bet was roughly right. We did get dramatically richer and more efficient. The hours part was spectacularly wrong. So what happened to all that freed-up time?

We spent it. Not literally, but close. As we got richer, our sense of "enough" climbed right along with our incomes. Keynes himself saw the risk: he split human wants into absolute needs (which can be satisfied) and relative needs, the ones that exist only because they make us feel ahead of other people. Those second ones, he wrote, may indeed be insatiable. The bigger house, the newer phone, the nicer trip: as soon as they are normal, they stop feeling like luxuries and start feeling like the baseline.

That is exactly the pattern we call [lifestyle creep]({{BASE}}blog/lifestyle-creep/): each raise quietly becomes the new normal, so the extra money buys a bigger life rather than a shorter work year. We took our productivity gains mostly as *more stuff* instead of *more time*. Keynes's grandchildren could have chosen the 15-hour week. We largely chose the bigger shopping cart.

(There is a romantic version of this story that says ancient hunter-gatherers already had the 15-hour week, the "original affluent society." It is a genuinely debated claim: later time-use studies of foraging groups tend to find more work once you count food processing, tool-making, and childcare. Treat the "cavemen only worked 15 hours" line as contested, not settled.)

## Does working 40 (or more) hours even pay off?

If 40 is not scientific, you would hope it is at least efficient. The honest answer: long hours are a lot less productive than they feel, especially at the top end.

One careful study by the economist John Pencavel looked at historical records of British munitions workers and found that output did not keep rising neatly with hours. Up to a point, more hours meant proportionally more output. Past a threshold (in that data, somewhere around a 49-hour week), extra hours added very little. Working much longer produced barely more than working moderately long. The extra, tired hours bought surprisingly little.

That fits what most of us already suspect about hour nine at a desk. So if very long hours are a poor deal, why does 40-plus persist?

- **The law is an anchor.** Once overtime pay kicks in above a set line, that line becomes the default shape of a "full-time" job. Employers design roles around it, and everyone calibrates to it.
- **Fixed costs per person.** Hiring, training, benefits, and management attention cost roughly the same whether someone works 30 hours or 45. That tempts employers to load more hours onto each existing head rather than hire more people.
- **Culture and identity.** "What do you do?" is usually the second question you get asked at a party. Busyness reads as importance. Long hours can feel like proof you matter, even when they are just proof you are tired.

None of this means work is bad. Work is also meaning, structure, and a paycheque. The point is narrower: the specific number 40 is a convention, and the "more hours equals more worth" instinct is not backed by the evidence.

## The money lens: price everything in hours of your life

Here is where the history becomes personal and genuinely useful. If your week is a fixed pile of hours that you trade for money, then every price tag has a hidden second number: how much of your life it costs. This idea was popularised by the book *Your Money or Your Life* by Vicki Robin and Joe Dominguez, and it is the most practical takeaway from everything above.

Most people think their hourly rate is "salary divided by paid hours." It is lower than that, usually by a lot, once you count what the job actually costs you in time and money.

### The Real-Hour Rule

Use this simple test to find your true hourly wage and then price things in it.

1. **Start with take-home pay**, the money that actually lands in your account, not the headline salary.
2. **Subtract the costs of working.** Commute fares or fuel, the "work wardrobe," bought lunches, the extra childcare that exists only because you are at work, and the decompression spending (the takeaway you order because you are too wiped to cook). These are real costs of the job.
3. **Divide by all the hours work eats.** Not just paid hours: add the commute, the getting-ready, the checking-email-in-bed, and the recovery time you need to be fit for the next day.
4. **That number is your real hourly wage.** Now flip it around: for any purchase, divide the price by your real hourly wage to see its price in life-hours.

Say Sam (our standard friendly example) takes home the equivalent of a 25-an-hour wage on paper. After the commute, the smart clothes, the sad desk salads, and the "I earned this" spending, Sam's real rate might be closer to 15 an hour. These are just illustrative numbers, but the gap is the point. A gadget priced at 300 is not "a bit of this month's pay." It is **20 hours of Sam's actual life**, half a working week, gone.

Once you see purchases that way, two things happen. Impulse buys lose some of their shine, because "two days of my life" is a stickier price than a number on a screen. And you stop quietly overpaying on the boring recurring stuff, because every unnecessary subscription or lazy renewal is now measured in hours, not pennies. (Overpaying out of inertia is its own trap; it is a cousin of the [loyalty penalty]({{BASE}}blog/loyalty-penalty/), where staying put quietly costs you.)

## Common myths and mistakes

- **Myth: 40 hours is scientifically optimal.** It is a bargained standard with a legal anchor, not a productivity finding. The evidence on very long hours actually points the other way.
- **Myth: more hours always means more done.** Past a point, output per hour falls. You can be busier and produce less.
- **Myth: Keynes was just wrong about everything.** He was largely right that we would get richer; arguably he underestimated how fast our wants would expand to absorb the gains.
- **Mistake: judging a job only by its salary.** A higher salary with a punishing commute and expensive "keeping up appearances" costs can be a worse real-hourly deal than a smaller one nearby. Do the Real-Hour maths before you compare.
- **Mistake: treating your hours as infinite.** Time is the one budget you cannot top up. Spending it like it is free is the most expensive habit there is.
- **Mistake: assuming your hours are legally fixed everywhere.** Rules on maximum hours, overtime, and flexible or part-time work vary a lot by country and employer. It is worth knowing what actually applies to you rather than guessing.

## What to do: put your hours to work

You cannot vote yourself a 15-hour week this afternoon. But you can change how you value and spend the hours you have. Here is a practical order of operations.

1. **Calculate your real hourly wage.** Run the Real-Hour Rule above once, properly, with honest numbers. Most people are quietly shocked, which is exactly the useful part.
2. **Convert your next big purchase into hours.** Before buying anything significant, divide the price by your real hourly wage. Then ask: is this thing worth that many hours of my life? Sometimes yes. Now it is a decision, not a reflex.
3. **Decide what extra hours are actually for.** If you take on overtime or a side hustle, name the goal before you start: a specific buffer, a debt cleared, a trip. Hours traded for a vague "more" tend to vanish into lifestyle creep. For more on how bright people still end up with nothing to show for good incomes, see [why smart people stay broke]({{BASE}}blog/why-smart-people-stay-broke/).
4. **Build a stash of "freedom money."** A cash buffer is not just insurance; it is leverage. Savings big enough to walk away from a bad arrangement for a while give you the standing to negotiate hours, push back on unpaid overtime, or wait for a better role instead of grabbing the first one.
5. **Ask about flexible or compressed hours.** Four longer days, a nine-day fortnight, part-time, or remote arrangements can buy back real time for the same or similar pay. The worst outcome of asking is usually a polite no. Plenty of people never ask and assume the answer.

## Frequently asked questions

### Who invented the 40-hour work week?

No single person did. It grew out of decades of labour campaigning for an eight-hour day, was adopted early by some large employers (Henry Ford's five-day week in the 1920s is the famous example), and was eventually written into law. In the US, the Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938 set the federal maximum and the overtime rule that cemented 40 as the norm.

### When did the 40-hour work week begin?

As a widespread legal standard, it dates to the late 1930s in the US: the Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938 set a maximum week (44 hours initially, dropping to 40 soon after) with premium pay above it. The *idea* is older, campaigned for through the 1800s and adopted by some firms in the 1920s, but the 1938 law is the usual answer to "when did it start."

### Is a 40-hour work week normal around the world?

It is a common anchor in many wealthier economies, but it is far from universal. International standards and regional rules differ: an early International Labour Organization convention aimed at eight hours a day and 48 a week in industry, and the EU's Working Time Directive caps average weekly hours at 48 including overtime. Actual hours worked vary widely by country, industry, and individual.

### Did Keynes really predict a 15-hour work week?

Yes. In his 1930 essay "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren," Keynes suggested that within roughly a century, rising wealth and productivity could reduce work to something like three-hour shifts or a 15-hour week. The productivity gains largely arrived; the leisure mostly did not. One common explanation is that our spending and our sense of "enough" expanded along with our incomes.

### Are longer working hours more productive?

Generally no, especially at the extremes. Research on historical working hours (notably John Pencavel's study of munitions workers) found that beyond a threshold, extra hours added very little output. Long hours can feel productive while producing surprisingly little, because fatigue drags down output per hour.

### How do I calculate the "real" hourly value of my time?

Take your actual take-home pay, subtract the money the job costs you (commuting, work clothes, bought lunches, work-driven childcare, decompression spending), then divide by *all* the hours work consumes, including commute, prep, and recovery, not only paid hours. The result is your real hourly wage, which you can then use to price purchases in hours of your life.
