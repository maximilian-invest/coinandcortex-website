Almost a hundred years ago, one of the most famous economists who ever lived predicted that his grandchildren would work about fifteen hours a week. We are, give or take, those grandchildren, and most of us are still doing something a lot closer to forty.

So what went wrong? He got the money part remarkably right and the time part remarkably wrong, and the reason why is a quiet lesson about your own pay rises, hiding inside a famous economics essay.

> **Short answer:** In a 1930 essay, John Maynard Keynes predicted that within about a hundred years people in rich countries would be four to eight times better off and might work only "three-hour shifts or a fifteen-hour week." The wealth prediction largely came true; the leisure one did not. We got far richer, then spent the gains on a bigger standard of living instead of a shorter week, because many of our wants are relative and climb as everyone else's do. The practical takeaway: productivity gains can be taken as time rather than stuff, but only if you decide to, on purpose.

## What did Keynes predict about work?

Keynes predicted that rising productivity would eventually solve what he called "the economic problem," the age-old grind of earning enough to live, so that his grandchildren would need to work only a small fraction of his own hours.

The essay is *Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren*. It began life as an after-dinner talk for schoolboys and then a public lecture; he published it in two parts in the magazine *The Nation and Athenaeum* in October of 1930, and later collected it in his book *Essays in Persuasion*. The timing is the surprising bit: he wrote it at the start of the Great Depression, surrounded by gloom, and set out to argue that the gloom was a passing phase, not the end of progress.

Stripped to its bones, the essay argues six things:

- Since the sixteenth century, capital accumulation, compound interest and invention had raised the average standard of life in Europe and the United States "about fourfold," and he expected that engine to keep running.
- He predicted that "the standard of life in progressive countries one hundred years hence will be between four and eight times as high" as in his day, "assuming no important wars and no important increase in population."
- He warned of "a new disease" called "technological unemployment," jobs lost because labour-saving inventions outrun new uses for labour, but treated it as "a temporary phase of maladjustment," not a disaster.
- With material needs largely met, he thought work would be shared out thinly, so that "three-hour shifts or a fifteen-hour week may put off the problem for a great while."
- He split human wants into two kinds, absolute and relative, and admitted that the relative ones "may indeed be insatiable."
- He hoped that, freed from scarcity, we would come to see "the love of money as a possession ... for what it is, a somewhat disgusting morbidity," and learn instead to live "wisely and agreeably and well."

If you want the wider story of how the working week actually settled at forty hours, that is the job of our pillar guide, [why we work 40 hours a week]({{BASE}}blog/why-do-we-work-40-hours-a-week/), which also sets out the Real-Hour Rule for pricing purchases in hours of your life. This piece stays on Keynes, because his small essay answers a very personal question: where did all the time go?

## A scorecard: right, wrong, and right in a way he never expected

Most write-ups treat Keynes as either a visionary or a fool. The fair verdict is more interesting, because he was strikingly right on one axis, plainly wrong on another, and quietly right on a third that almost nobody checks.

| Prediction | What actually happened | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Incomes rise four to eight times in a century | UK real GDP per person reached a little over five times its early-1930s level within about eight decades; average world income per person rose roughly fourfold in the second half of the twentieth century | Right |
| People work about 15 hours a week | No country is close; UK men's annual market hours fell by only a little over a quarter | Wrong |
| Free time expands a great deal | Expected lifetime leisure and home-production hours for a UK man rose about 58% | Right, but mostly in retirement |
| Material needs largely satisfied | The demand for quality proved effectively bottomless; relative wants kept rising | Wrong |

**On income, he nailed it.** The economic historian Nicholas Crafts, revisiting the prediction, finds UK real GDP per person reached a little over five times its early-1930s level, comfortably inside Keynes's four-to-eight band, even though Britain grew more slowly than most. Globally, Fabrizio Zilibotti calculates that population-weighted world income per person grew about fourfold in the second half of the twentieth century, hitting the bottom of Keynes's hundred-year range in half the time. And as Richard Freeman notes, growth of about 2% a year, compounded over a century, produces roughly the eightfold gain at the top of the band.

**On the weekly hours, he missed.** Hours did fall. Annual working hours in the early-industrialised countries have roughly halved since the late nineteenth century, by nearly 60% in Germany and around 40% in the UK, according to Our World in Data. But they flattened out far above fifteen a week: Crafts notes that UK men's annual hours of paid work fell by a little over a quarter, nowhere near the two-thirds drop a fifteen-hour week would require.

**And here is the part the headlines miss.** Count a whole life instead of a single week and Keynes looks better than his reputation. Crafts estimates that a twenty-year-old British man's expected lifetime hours of leisure and home production rose by about 58%, so the share of his waking hours spent in paid work fell from 38.5% to 22.8%. On reasonable assumptions, Crafts argues, that is more than Keynes himself would have predicted. The twist is where it landed: not in shorter weeks, but in far longer retirements, because people now live much longer after they stop. Zilibotti reaches the same place from another angle, estimating that a stylised "Keynes forecast" life spends about 7.6% of its waking hours in paid work, against 14.4% on a thirty-hour week or 18.3% on a thirty-eight-hour one in a modern rich-world life. Time-use diaries point the same way in the US: across the decades of surveys Mark Aguiar and Erik Hurst studied, leisure rose by about 6 to 8 hours a week for men and 4 to 8 for women, even though market hours per working-age adult stayed relatively stable. We did win a lot of our time back. Much of it simply arrived at the end of life, in one long block, and the rest came as modest weekly gains rather than the dramatic short week he pictured.

## Why didn't Keynes's prediction come true?

Because he underestimated how much our wants would grow, and overestimated how willingly we would swap money for time once comfortable. The book *Revisiting Keynes*, edited by Lorenzo Pecchi and Gustavo Piga, gathered leading economists (four of them Nobel laureates) to chew on exactly this puzzle, and a Bureau of Labor Statistics review of a later reassessment, by economist Jörg Bibow, lists six plausible explanations, from simply preferring consumption to leisure to inequality leaving basic needs unmet. In our reading, four forces do most of the explaining.

### Rising wages made free time expensive

When your pay goes up, two opposite pulls act on you. Being richer makes you want more rest (the income effect), but every hour of rest now costs more in foregone earnings (the substitution effect). Keynes, like most economists of his day, assumed the first pull would win. Freeman argues it was the second that won, especially for higher earners: the better the pay, the more a free afternoon "costs." The "idle rich" of Keynes's era, who worked little because they could, were replaced by what Freeman calls the "workaholic rich": higher earners who now tend to work more hours than the lower paid.

### We compete, so many wants are relative

Keynes saw this danger and then waved it away. He granted that needs which are "relative in the sense that we feel them only if their satisfaction lifts us above, makes us feel superior to, our fellows" may indeed be insatiable, "for the higher the general level, the higher still are they," then assumed they were a minor part of spending. The evidence suggests otherwise. Samuel Bowles and Yongjin Park, studying ten advanced economies, found that greater earnings inequality goes hand in hand with longer work hours, an effect they trace to Thorstein Veblen's idea that we emulate the consumption of the rich. Their estimate is sizeable: a one-standard-deviation rise in the gap between high and middle earners lines up with about 3.4% more annual hours. When the people above you pull away, you run harder to keep the gap from widening.

### "Enough" keeps moving, because quality is relative too

The economist Robert Frank argues Keynes made a subtler error: he thought context mattered only for show-off goods, when in fact it shapes almost everything, including things that feel purely practical. A "memorable meal," a "good" car, a "safe" neighbourhood with "good" schools: each standard is set by what is around you, and each ratchets up as incomes rise. Frank's conclusion is blunt. The demand for quality is "universal and inexhaustible," so no level of productivity will ever let most people buy "everything they ever wanted." That is lifestyle creep at the scale of a whole society: each of us upgrades sensibly, and together we move the finish line for everyone. We cover the personal version, and a rule to protect your next raise, in the [lifestyle creep]({{BASE}}blog/lifestyle-creep/) guide and its Raise Rule.

### We have to pay for longer retirements

This one is less about psychology than arithmetic. Crafts points out that the same longer lifespans that gave us all that extra leisure also created a bill: decades of retirement to finance. Expecting a longer, costlier retirement is a strong reason to work and save hard while you can, not to trim your week to fifteen hours. The freedom arrived with a savings target attached.

## The Enough Line: how to take some gains as time, not stuff

Keynes handed us a usable tool without quite meaning to. He split wants into **absolute** needs, the ones you would feel on a desert island, and **relative** needs, which only bite because of the people around you. Frank added a third, call it context creep: spending that feels purely practical but is quietly set by your surroundings. Sort your recurring spending into those three buckets and you draw your Enough Line, the point past which more money mostly buys a faster treadmill. Here is how to run it.

1. **List your regular spending in three buckets.** *Absolute:* housing, basic food, heating, getting to work, essential care. *Relative:* the parts chosen partly so you are not seen to fall behind, like the newer car on finance or the brand that signals you are doing fine. *Context creep:* upgrades that feel like plain good sense but have crept with your income, such as the "good" neighbourhood premium, the quality tier you now assume, the standard of holiday you no longer question.
2. **Price the top two buckets in hours.** Divide the monthly total of your relative and context-creep spending by your real hourly wage (the Real-Hour Rule from the [pillar]({{BASE}}blog/why-do-we-work-40-hours-a-week/)). That is how many hours a month you work purely to stay level with the context around you.
3. **Find your own Enough Line.** Ask of each relative or context item the Re-Buy Test from our [lifestyle creep examples]({{BASE}}blog/lifestyle-creep-examples/) guide: if I did not already have this, would I sign up for it today, at today's price? A clear no sits below your Enough Line.
4. **Decide the split before your next raise.** This is the Keynes move at household scale. Choose, in advance, what share of a pay rise becomes time rather than more spending. Deciding beforehand beats deciding when the "I deserve this" voice is already shopping.

Take Sam, our standing hypothetical, whose real hourly wage works out at about 20 in whatever your currency is (an example figure). Over a few raises, Sam's relative and context-creep spending has drifted up by roughly 400 a month: a smarter car, premium everything, the upgraded version of things that worked fine. Divide 400 by 20 and that is 20 hours of work a month, close to one afternoon off every week. Sam was not reckless, and could not have named the upgrades without adding them up. But living at the standard of a few raises ago would, in effect, buy back that afternoon: the trade Keynes thought we would all take.

## Common myths and mistakes

- **Myth: Keynes was simply wrong.** He was right about income growth, right that free time would expand a lot, and wrong mainly about the shape it took. He framed it loosely himself, writing that a fifteen-hour week "may put off the problem for a great while," not that it was a certainty. A hundred years after the essay has not fully arrived, but no country is anywhere near fifteen hours regardless.
- **Myth: he was writing about everyone.** By his own framing he was mostly describing men's paid work in "progressive countries," assuming no major wars and little population growth. Married women's move into paid work, which he did not foresee, reshaped the whole picture.
- **Mistake: thinking a raise automatically buys freedom.** Without a decision, extra income tends to raise your baseline instead, the central trap in [why smart people stay broke]({{BASE}}blog/why-smart-people-stay-broke/).
- **Mistake: assuming the choice is purely yours.** Many employers do not offer genuinely pro-rata part-time work, and fixed costs like housing and care do not shrink just because you want fewer hours. The Enough Line shows you the trade; it cannot promise your employer will cooperate.

## What to do: trade some productivity for time

You cannot vote yourself a fifteen-hour week this afternoon. But you can capture a slice of Keynes's gains as time rather than stuff. Beyond running the Enough Line above, three moves matter most.

1. **Pre-commit your next raise.** Decide what share converts to time, not spending, before the money lands. Even one fewer working day a month adds up: twelve days a year over a forty-year career is close to two full working years (example arithmetic).
2. **Ask for the time directly.** Compressed hours, a four-day week, unpaid leave, an earlier finish. The worst answer is usually a polite no, and some of these cost little or nothing in pay.
3. **Build a buffer, because time needs leverage.** Savings big enough to walk away for a while are what let you negotiate hours or decline unpaid overtime. If money keeps vanishing before you can save it, [why am I always broke]({{BASE}}blog/why-am-i-always-broke/) helps you find the leak.

None of this makes you Keynes's fully liberated grandchild. It just means you take part of the bargain he promised, deliberately, instead of letting it dissolve into a slightly nicer version of the same busy life.

## Key terms

- **Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren:** Keynes's short 1930 essay predicting that productivity growth would, within about a century, make rich countries several times wealthier and allow a roughly fifteen-hour week.
- **Absolute needs:** In Keynes's terms, wants you feel regardless of anyone else, such as food, warmth and shelter, which can in principle be satisfied.
- **Relative needs:** Wants that bite only because they make you feel ahead of, or not behind, other people; Keynes thought these "may indeed be insatiable."
- **Income effect:** The pull toward more leisure as you get richer, because you can afford to work less.
- **Substitution effect:** The pull toward more work as wages rise, because each hour of leisure now costs more in foregone pay.
- **Veblen effect:** The tendency to emulate the spending of richer people, named after Thorstein Veblen; linked by Bowles and Park to longer work hours where inequality is higher.
- **The Enough Line:** Our framework for sorting recurring spending into absolute, relative and context-creep buckets, pricing the last two in work-hours, and deciding in advance how much of a raise to take as time.

## Frequently asked questions

### What did Keynes predict about the work week?

In his 1930 essay *Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren*, he predicted that within about a hundred years rising productivity would make rich countries four to eight times wealthier and could reduce work to "three-hour shifts or a fifteen-hour week." He thought most people would still want some work, just far less of it, once their material needs were met.

### Why didn't the 15-hour week happen?

Because our wants grew along with our incomes. Higher wages made leisure more expensive to take (the substitution effect), many needs are relative and rise with everyone else's spending, the standard for "good quality" keeps climbing, and longer retirements gave us a reason to work and save hard while young. We took the gains mainly as a bigger standard of living, not a shorter week.

### What is Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren about?

It is Keynes's argument, written at the start of the Great Depression, that the economic problem of scarcity was temporary and nearly solved. He forecast large income gains, warned of short-term "technological unemployment," imagined a future of abundant leisure, and worried we might struggle to use it well. It later appeared in his book *Essays in Persuasion*.

### Was Keynes right about anything?

Yes, about the big economic trend. Crafts finds UK real GDP per person rose to a little over five times its early-1930s level, inside Keynes's four-to-eight band. Counting a whole lifetime, expected leisure and home-production hours rose about 58% in his estimate, more than Keynes implied. The weekly-hours forecast is the famous miss.

### Will we ever work 15 hours a week?

Not soon, and no country is close. The forces that blocked it, relative wants, the high cost of taking leisure, and the need to fund long retirements, are still in place. A fifteen-hour standard would require a large shift in both policy and in what we treat as "enough."

### Could I work 15 hours a week?

A few people engineer something close by keeping spending near their absolute needs and saving aggressively, but it is out of reach for most, and honestly so. Many employers do not offer truly pro-rata part-time work, and fixed costs like rent and care do not fall just because your hours do. The realistic version is capturing part of the gain: pricing your relative spending in hours, and converting a share of future raises into time rather than stuff.
