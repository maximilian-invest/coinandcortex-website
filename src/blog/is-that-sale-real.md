You know the feeling. A number sits crossed out on the tag, a smaller number sits beside it, and something in your chest relaxes: *this is a good deal, I should grab it before it goes.* You might even know, intellectually, that the crossed-out number is a sales tactic. It works anyway. That gap, between knowing it is a trick and still feeling the pull, is not a flaw in you. It is the entire design.

This article is the buyer's field guide to that trick: what the crossed-out number is doing to your brain, what the rules in different places say a "real" former price has to be, and a fast check to decide whether a sale is worth it.

> **Short answer:** A sale is "real" only if the higher reference price was a genuine, recent selling price, not a number invented to make the discount look big. The crossed-out "was" price, the RRP, the "compare at" tag, and the outlet "retail value" ticket all work by anchoring: the first number you see drags your sense of a fair price up, so the second number feels like a win. The fix is to ignore the anchor and judge the price you are actually paying on its own, against the recent real price and one other seller.

## Why a crossed-out number works even when you know it's a trick

The short version: your brain does not price things from scratch, it prices them relative to whatever number it saw first. Show it a high number, and every number after that looks smaller. Researchers call this anchoring, and it is one of the most stubborn findings in the study of how we judge value.

The classic demonstration is almost silly. Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman spun a wheel of fortune, secretly rigged to stop on either 10 or 65, then asked people what percentage of countries in the United Nations are African. People who saw 10 guessed about 25 percent. People who saw 65 guessed about 45 percent. A random number from a wheel, which everyone knew was random, dragged their answers twenty points apart. The anchor did not need to be relevant, or credible, or even chosen. It just had to be seen first.

Prices are more vulnerable to this than UN trivia, because you genuinely do not know what most things "should" cost. Dan Ariely, George Loewenstein and Drazen Prelec showed how far that goes: they had people write down the last two digits of their social security number, then bid on items like a keyboard. People whose digits fell in the top group would pay around 56 dollars for a cordless keyboard; people in the bottom group, around 16. A number with no meaning, pulled from an ID, reshaped what felt like a fair price for the same object.

And before you think this is a trap only for the inattentive: Gregory Northcraft and Margaret Neale took professional estate agents to a real house, gave different agents different listing prices for it, and watched their valuations drift with the arbitrary number on the sheet. Most of the agents insisted the listing price had not influenced them at all. Experienced professionals, valuing a real house in their own market, anchored too, and could not feel it happening.

That is the uncomfortable core of it. Anchoring runs underneath the part of you that "knows better," which is exactly why a crossed-out price keeps working after you read an article like this one. ## 10 anchoring pricing examples you actually meet

Anchors are not one trick, they are a family of them, and once you can name each one it loses most of its grip. Here is the scannable version: what each looks like, the one-line reason it works, and the tell that gives it away.

| The anchor | What it looks like | Why it works | The tell |
|---|---|---|---|
| The "was" price | A big crossed-out number beside a smaller one | First number sets your reference point | The "was" is round, vague, or you never saw it live |
| RRP / MSRP / list price | "RRP 80, our price 40" on goods never sold at 80 | A maker's suggested price feels official | Nobody actually charges the RRP |
| "Compare at" | "Compare at 120" with no seller named | An unnamed rival you can't check | There is no link, store, or date to compare against |
| Outlet "retail value" | A ticket showing a high "value" and a low price | Implies the item once cost the high number | Some outlet goods are made for the outlet, never sold elsewhere |
| The decoy up top | A very pricey option shown first on a menu or page | It makes the next option look moderate | The dear item exists mainly to be skipped |
| "Up to 70% off" | A huge headline discount across a sale | You picture the biggest cut, not the typical one | "Up to" means one item; most are barely reduced |
| "Worth 50" bonus | A free gift or bundle valued at a flashy number | A fake value anchor for something near-worthless | The "worth" price is one nobody would pay |
| The pre-sale hike | A price quietly raised, then "discounted" | The inflated price becomes the anchor | The "sale" price matches last month's normal price |
| Countdown / "2 left" | A timer or stock warning beside the deal | Urgency stops you checking the anchor | The timer resets, or the stock never runs out |
| Price per unit hidden | A bigger pack that looks like bulk value | "Bigger must be cheaper" anchors you | The small pack is cheaper per unit when you divide |

Two of these deserve a closer look, because they cost the most.

The **pre-sale price hike** is the engine behind a lot of "permanent" sales. A thing sits at a normal price, gets bumped up for a short spell, then reappears "reduced" to roughly where it always was. The high number you are measuring against was never a real selling price; it was built to be crossed out. This is precisely the behavior the rules further down try to catch.

The **first number in a negotiation** is the same trick wearing a suit. The opening salary a recruiter floats, the sticker on a car, the contractor's first quote: each is an anchor dropped on purpose, because the first number on the table tends to pull the final one toward it. The defense is having your own number ready before you hear theirs.

A quick, clearly hypothetical case. Sam wants a jacket. The tag says "was 200, now 120," a tidy 40 percent off, and the urge to buy is immediate. But a price-history check shows the jacket sat at 130 for months, popped up to 200 for two weeks, then "dropped" to 120. The real recent price was 130, so the genuine saving is 10, not 80. A second shop has the same jacket at 115. The 80-off bargain was mostly a story told with a crossed-out number. (All example figures, in whatever your currency is.)

## What counts as a real "was" price (US, EU and UK)

Here is the reassuring part: a lot of places have decided this is worth regulating, because fake former prices are old and common. The rules differ, enforcement varies, and this is general information rather than legal advice, but the underlying idea is strikingly similar everywhere. A former price only counts if it was a real price, really charged, for a real stretch of time.

| Where | The core idea | What it means for you |
|---|---|---|
| US | A former price must be bona fide, not invented to be slashed | A "was" price the item barely sold at can be deceptive |
| EU | An announced reduction must show the prior price | The "before" price is the lowest of the previous 30-plus days |
| UK | Reference prices must not mislead | A "was" price should reflect a genuine recent selling price |

In the US, the Federal Trade Commission's guides against deceptive pricing put it plainly. A former price is legitimate if it "is the actual, bona fide price at which the article was offered to the public on a regular basis for a reasonably substantial period of time." The guide even spells out the scam with a pen: advertising "Terrific Bargain: X Pens, Were $10, Now Only $7.50!" is deceptive if that 10 was an inflated figure the pens never really sold at. The test is not the size of the discount, it is whether the higher price was ever genuinely yours to pay.

In the EU, the price indication rules take the guesswork out with a number. When a seller announces a price reduction, the "prior price" they show you has to be the lowest price they charged during a period of at least 30 days before the discount. That design makes the classic bump-it-up-then-slash-it move much harder: you cannot inflate a price for a few days and call the drop a saving, because the reference has to reach back across the month.

In the UK, the competition regulator's guidance on unfair commercial practices treats a misleading reference price as exactly that, misleading. The spirit is the same: a "was" price should reflect a genuine recent selling price, not a number conjured to flatter the deal. Across all three, notice what the law does not promise. A sale can be perfectly legal and still be a bad buy for you, because the rules police the seller's maths, not whether you actually want the thing.

## The Full-Price Test: one question that pops the anchor

You do not need to memorize ten tricks in the moment. You need one question that ignores the anchor entirely and puts the decision back where it belongs, on the price you are actually about to pay.

> **The Full-Price Test:** cover the crossed-out number and ask, "Would I buy this, at the price I'm paying, if no 'was' price were shown at all?"

If the honest answer is yes, the discount is a bonus and you can buy with a clear head. If the answer is "well, only because it's on sale," that is the anchor talking, not you, and it is a signal to stop. The test works because it strips out the comparison the seller planted and forces an absolute judgment: is this number, by itself, worth it to me? A real deal survives that question. A manufactured one falls apart the second you hide the anchor.

It pairs with a simple truth worth saying out loud: a discount is not money you made. Spending 120 to "save 80" still costs you 120. You only truly save by not spending, or by paying less for something you were always going to buy anyway.

## The 60-second sale check, step by step

When a sale tempts you, run this. It takes about a minute and it defuses almost every anchor above.

1. **Ignore the crossed-out number completely.** Treat the "was," the RRP, and the "compare at" as decoration. They are the anchor; your job is to not look at them. Read only the price you would actually pay.
2. **Check the recent real price.** Look at the price history, or your own memory of what this item has cost lately. The question is "what has this genuinely sold for over the past weeks," not "what does the tag claim it used to be." A browser extension or price-history view does this in seconds; this is step three of our [Fair Price Protocol]({{BASE}}blog/fair-price-protocol/), trust the price history, not the sale badge.
3. **Compare one other seller.** Pull up the same item, same size and spec, at a second shop. One comparison is usually enough to tell a real low from a dressed-up one.
4. **Decide on the price alone.** Now run the Full-Price Test. Would you pay today's number if there were no sale at all? Yes, and it beats the other seller and the recent real price, buy it. Otherwise, walk, and let the countdown timer count down without you.

The whole point is to replace a feeling ("this is a deal") with three facts: recent price, rival price, your own ceiling. Facts do not anchor.

## Common myths and mistakes

**Myth: a bigger discount means a better deal.** The percentage off tells you nothing on its own, because it is measured against a number the seller chose. "70% off" an inflated price can be dearer than "10% off" an honest one. Judge the final price, never the size of the cut.

**Myth: "outlet" means the same product, cheaper.** Some outlet stock is made specifically for outlets and never sold at the "retail value" on the ticket. That high value is a pure anchor; there is often no regular store where the item sold for it.

**Mistake: trusting the deadline.** "Sale ends tonight" and "only 2 left" exist to stop you running the 60-second check. You rarely can check whether a timer or a "low stock" warning is real, and some simply reset. Urgency is a reason to slow down, not speed up.

**Mistake: thinking "I saved money" when you spent it.** A saving you only unlocked by buying something is not a saving, it is a purchase. The money left your account. This is one of the quiet reasons capable people stay strapped, explored in [why smart people stay broke]({{BASE}}blog/why-smart-people-stay-broke/).

**Mistake: letting "only X a month" hide the total.** Financing re-anchors you on a small monthly figure so the full price disappears from view. Always add it up. The real question is whether the total, plus any interest, passes the Full-Price Test.

## Key terms

- **Anchoring:** The tendency to judge a number relative to the first number you saw, so a high reference price makes a lower price feel cheap.
- **Reference price:** A comparison number a seller shows you, such as a "was" price, RRP, or "compare at," against which the current price is measured.
- **Former price (bona fide price):** A previous price that was genuinely charged, openly and for a real period, so a comparison against it is honest.
- **RRP / MSRP:** A recommended or suggested retail price set by a maker, often well above what anyone actually charges, used as a flattering anchor.
- **Fictitious pricing:** Inventing or inflating a former price purely to make a later "discount" look larger than it is.
- **Decoy:** A deliberately pricey or poor-value option placed to make another option look reasonable by comparison.
- **The Full-Price Test:** Our one question: would I buy this, at the price I'm paying, if no crossed-out price were shown at all?

## Frequently asked questions

### How can I tell if a sale is real?

Ignore the crossed-out number and check two things: what the item has genuinely sold for recently, and what one other seller charges for the same thing right now. If today's price beats both, the sale is real and worth it. If the "discount" just brings the price back to its normal recent level, the big saving is mostly a story told with an inflated reference number.

### What is a fake discount?

A fake discount is a price reduction measured against a former price that was never a genuine, regular selling price. The usual method is to raise a price briefly, or to print an RRP nobody charges, then cross it out so the "sale" price looks like a bargain. The current price might be ordinary or even high; only the comparison is invented.

### Is anchoring bias illegal?

Anchoring itself is just how human judgment works, so it cannot be banned. What some places regulate is the dishonest use of reference prices. In the US, a former price must be one genuinely charged for a reasonable period; in the EU, an advertised reduction must be measured against the lowest price of the prior 30-plus days. Rules differ by place, and a legal sale can still be a poor deal for you.

### What does "compare at" or RRP actually mean?

Both are reference prices, not prices anyone necessarily charges. An RRP or MSRP is a maker's suggested figure, frequently set high so retailers can advertise a discount from it. "Compare at" is even looser, since it often names no seller you can check. Treat both as anchors to ignore, and compare against a real, current price from an actual shop instead.

### Why does everything seem to be on sale all the time?

Because a permanent "sale" is a permanent anchor. If a product almost always shows a crossed-out higher price, that higher price is doing marketing work, not describing a real recent cost. The steady "discount" makes the ordinary price feel like a deal every day, which is exactly why it never ends. Judge the price you pay, not the badge.

### Does knowing about anchoring make me immune to it?

Not reliably. The estate agents in the research knew their jobs cold and still drifted with an arbitrary listing price, while denying it affected them. Awareness helps a little, but the sturdier defense is a process that removes the anchor from the decision, like covering the "was" price and running the Full-Price Test, rather than trusting yourself to resist it in the moment.
